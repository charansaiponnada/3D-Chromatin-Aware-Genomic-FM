# BeHuman

A hard self-edit pass, run against every piece of substantive prose, that removes the stylistic fingerprints AI writing tends to leave behind. Sourced from Wikipedia's "Signs of AI Writing" field guide (WikiProject AI Cleanup) plus the patterns practitioners have converged on since.

## How to use this skill

1. Write the draft normally first. Don't try to self-censor mid-sentence.
2. Run the full checklist below against the draft as a distinct editing pass.
3. For every hit, rewrite the sentence with a concrete, specific claim in its place — don't just delete the flag and leave a gap. If there's nothing specific to say, that's a sign the sentence wasn't earning its place anyway; cut it.
4. Re-read once more for rhythm. Removing every flagged pattern can leave writing choppy — smooth the transitions in your own words, don't reinsert the banned crutch.

The rule of thumb: if a sentence could be dropped into an article about almost any other topic and still sound plausible, it's the AI-smoothing effect. Replace it with something that could only be true of this specific subject.

## Checklist A — Puffery & significance inflation

Ban these phrases and their close variants outright:

- "stands as a testament to," "serves as a reminder of"
- "plays a pivotal / crucial / vital / significant role in"
- "underscores / highlights / reflects the importance / significance of"
- "a watershed moment," "a pivotal moment"
- "leaves a lasting impact / legacy," "enduring legacy"
- "rich cultural heritage," "rich tapestry," "deeply rooted in"
- "continues to captivate," "continues to inspire"
- "in the ever-evolving landscape of," "in today's fast-paced world"

Fix: state what actually happened, with a specific mechanism, date, number, or name. If you can't say why something mattered in concrete terms, don't claim it mattered.

## Checklist B — Fake analysis via dangling participles

Watch for sentences that tack on a vague "-ing" clause instead of making a real claim:

- "...highlighting its importance"
- "...underscoring its significance"
- "...reflecting broader trends"
- "...paving the way for future developments"
- "...solidifying its place in [X]"

Fix: either turn it into a full, specific sentence with a real claim, or delete it. A trailing gerund clause is almost never doing actual work.

## Checklist C — Vague attribution / weasel words

- "some critics argue," "many believe," "experts say," "industry reports suggest," "it is widely believed," "observers have noted," "studies show" (with no study named)

Fix: name the actual source. If you don't have one, don't manufacture the appearance of consensus — say what you know and stop.

## Checklist D — Hedging that adds nothing

- "may," "could," "often considered," "results may vary," "it's worth noting that," "it should be noted that," "generally speaking" used as filler rather than a real qualifier

Fix: if you're confident, say the thing plainly. If you're genuinely uncertain, say what you're uncertain about specifically, not with a generic hedge word.

## Checklist E — Hollow openers, transitions, and conclusions

- Openers: "In today's world," "In the world of X," "When it comes to X,"
- Transitions used mechanically: "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "That said," stacked one after another as connective tissue rather than because the logic demands them
- Conclusions: "In conclusion," "In summary," "Overall," "At the end of the day," "Ultimately," when they just restate what was already said

Fix: cut the opener and start with the actual point. Cut restated conclusions entirely — if the piece needs a close, close on a specific implication, not a summary label.

## Checklist F — Rhetorical set-pieces

- Negative parallelism: "It's not just X, it's Y" / "This isn't about X — it's about Y"
- Engagement bait: "Let that sink in," "Read that again," "This changes everything," "Here's the thing:"
- Rule-of-three overload: three adjectives, three examples, or three clauses used reflexively as a rhythm device rather than because three genuinely distinct things exist
- Rhetorical questions used as section openers or transitions ("But what does this mean for X?")

Fix: say the thing directly once. If a real contrast exists, state both sides plainly without the "not X, it's Y" scaffold.

## Checklist G — Formatting tells

- Reflexive bullet points or numbered lists where prose would read more naturally — lists are fine when items are genuinely parallel and the user benefits from scanning them, not as a default structure
- Excessive bolding of key terms inside running prose
- Title Case Headings used where sentence case is the norm for the context
- Emoji used decoratively in headers or as bullet markers
- Boilerplate section headers appended out of habit ("Conclusion," "Challenges," "Future Outlook," "Key Takeaways") when the content doesn't actually need that scaffolding
- Curly/smart quotes and apostrophes where the surrounding context uses straight ones (or vice versa) — stay consistent with what's already in play

Fix: default to plain prose. Reach for a list only when the content is genuinely enumerable and scanning it is more useful than reading it as sentences.

## Checklist H — Generic smoothing / statistical regression to the mean

- Descriptions that could be swapped onto a different subject with minimal edits and still sound plausible ("a testament to innovation and creativity," "known for its vibrant culture and rich history")
- Marketing adjectives applied by default: "stunning," "breathtaking," "state-of-the-art," "must-see," "vibrant," "bustling," "nestled"
- Overexplaining or restating a point that was just made a sentence earlier
- Every paragraph opening with the same sentence structure

Fix: replace generic praise with one specific, checkable fact. If you can't find one, the claim probably doesn't belong in the piece.

## Checklist I — Leftover AI artifacts

Only relevant if editing pasted AI output rather than writing from scratch, but check anyway:

- Meta-commentary that slipped through: "As an AI...", "I hope this helps!", "Let me know if you'd like me to adjust this"
- Unfilled template brackets or placeholder instructions
- Cheerful sign-offs that don't match the register of the piece

Fix: delete on sight. These should never survive to a final draft.

## Calibration notes

- These are strong defaults, not a search for false positives to avoid at all costs. Cut ruthlessly first, then sanity-check that the rewrite still reads naturally.
- A pattern appearing once isn't automatically wrong — a genuine numbered list of five parallel steps, or one honest "furthermore" where the logic actually calls for it, is fine. What this skill targets is the reflexive, default use of these patterns everywhere, which is what makes writing read as generated rather than composed.
- When in doubt, prefer the version with more concrete nouns, numbers, and names, and fewer abstract nouns and adjectives.
